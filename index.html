What you migrate to

NIST's finalised post-quantum standards · published 13 Aug 2024

FIPS 203 · ML-KEMGeneral encryption and key exchange — the replacement for RSA and ECDH.
FIPS 204 · ML-DSAPrimary digital signatures — the replacement for RSA and ECDSA.
FIPS 205 · SLH-DSAHash-based backup signatures, using different maths for resilience.

A backup KEM, HQC, was selected in March 2025 (final ~2027); FIPS 206 (FN-DSA / Falcon) is in draft. In plain terms: ML-KEM and ML-DSA are the defaults; the others are fallbacks if one maths family is ever broken.

Key & signature sizes: classical vs post-quantum

Bytes on the wire · log scale · tap a bar for a plain-terms explanation

Why size matters: these bytes travel in every TLS handshake, certificate chain and signed firmware image. An ML-DSA-65 signature is ~50× an ECDSA one, so handshakes that fitted in a single network packet now span several — slowing connections, breaking fixed-size buffers in older hardware and protocols, and straining constrained OT devices and low-bandwidth links. The size gap, not the maths, is where most migration pain lives — which is why interoperability testing and crypto-agility sit at the heart of every national migration plan.

Classical (RSA/ECC) Post-quantum (NIST)
Post-quantum keys and signatures are far larger — an ML-DSA-65 signature is ~50× an ECDSA one, and an SLH-DSA signature is ~17KB (≈260× RSA-2048). That inflates TLS handshakes, certificate chains and firmware images, and can break assumptions in older hardware, protocols and buffers — which is why interoperability testing and crypto-agility matter.

How the Cryptography Works — and What Replaces It

A journey in eight steps — open each in turn (or jump around): why it matters, how today’s locks work, how they’re managed, why quantum changes everything, and what comes next. Two ideas protect almost everything online — RSA and elliptic curves. Both rest on maths that is easy in one direction and (classically) hopeless in reverse. Shor’s algorithm reverses both. Here’s how they work, in plain English and in the maths, and what the new NIST FIPS standards replace them with.

You have probably used both a dozen times before breakfast — this is why Q-Day is an everyone problem, not a cryptographer problem:

🔒 The browser padlockTLS

Every HTTPS page starts with an elliptic-curve key exchange, inside a certificate chain signed with RSA or ECDSA. Billions of handshakes a day.

💬 MessagingE2E

WhatsApp, Signal and iMessage agree keys with elliptic curves — Signal and iMessage now layer post-quantum on top (SPQR and PQ3).

💳 Cards & bankingEMV

The chip in your bank card authenticates itself to the terminal with RSA; online banking rides the same TLS as the padlock.

📲 Software updatescode signing

Your phone and laptop only install updates whose signatures verify — today mostly RSA/ECDSA. Forge those and you own the device fleet.

🛂 Passports & IDeMRTD

The chip in a biometric passport proves it’s genuine with digital signatures — documents that must stay valid for a decade.

⚡ Energy & OTCNI

Smart meters, substation gateways and SCADA links authenticate with device certificates — on hardware that lives 15–30 years. The shelf-life problem in physical form.

In plain English: pick two large secret prime numbers and multiply them. Anyone can see the result; nobody can feasibly split it back into the two primes. Multiplying is seconds; un-multiplying a 2,048-bit number would take classical computers longer than the age of the universe. Your secret is the ingredients — the world only ever sees the cake.

Tap a number and try to factor it.

The maths: the public key is N = p×q (plus an exponent e); the private key d satisfies e·d ≡ 1 mod φ(N). Encryption is c = me mod N; only knowing p and q lets you compute d and reverse it. Security = the difficulty of factoring N. The largest number ever factored by open classical effort is RSA-250 (829 bits, months of supercomputer time) — RSA-2048 is astronomically beyond that. Shor’s algorithm factors N in polynomial time, collapsing the entire construction once a large fault-tolerant quantum computer exists.

In plain English: imagine bouncing a billiard ball on a strangely curved table using one fixed rule. Everyone knows the starting spot and the rule. Your secret is simply how many times you bounced. Given the final position, working out the number of bounces is (classically) as hopeless as factoring — but the keys are far smaller, which is why your phone, bank and TLS use elliptic curves.

k = 1 · public point P

The maths: points on y² = x³ + ax + b (over a finite field) form a group: a chord through two points, reflected, gives their “sum”. The private key is a number k; the public key is Q = kP. Recovering k from Q is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem — 256-bit ECC gives security comparable to 3,072-bit RSA. Shor’s discrete-log variant breaks this too: Google’s 2026 estimates put 256-bit ECC within reach of ~500,000 physical superconducting qubits, and a March 2026 Oratomic/Caltech paper argues P-256 discrete logs could take days on ~26,000 neutral-atom qubits. The neat twist: NIST’s key-management bible is being rewritten right now — the draft SP 800-57 Rev 6 (Dec 2025) folds the post-quantum algorithms of FIPS 203/204/205 into the same lifecycle rules. Good classical key management and PQC migration are becoming one discipline. SP 800-57 Rev 6 draft ↗

Both locks share one weakness: they’re only hard classically. In 1994 Peter Shor showed a quantum computer runs both trapdoors backwards — factoring and curve-unwinding — in polynomial time. No key length saves them; only different mathematics does.

And you don’t get to wait for the machine: traffic and data recorded today can be decrypted the day it arrives — harvest now, decrypt later. That’s why every deadline in this app is dated years before anyone expects the computer itself.

Requirements keep falling: Gidney (2025) cut RSA-2048 to under a million noisy qubits; Google’s 2026 estimates put 256-bit ECC near ~500k; and the March 2026 Oratomic/Caltech paper argues ~10,000 reconfigurable neutral-atom qubits could suffice — with P-256 discrete logs in days at ~26,000. Track it live on the Trends tab. Cain et al. ↗ Gidney ↗

In plain English: in August 2024, after an eight-year global competition, NIST published the replacement locks. Instead of factoring or curve-bouncing, they hide secrets inside noisy high-dimensional lattices (imagine finding one specific point in a 500-dimensional haystack) or inside chains of hash functions. No known quantum shortcut exists for either.

FIPS 203 · ML-KEMkey exchange

The workhorse (from CRYSTALS-Kyber). Replaces RSA and elliptic-curve key exchange — already shipping in hybrid TLS at Cloudflare, AWS, Apple and Signal. Public keys 800–1,568 bytes.

FIPS 204 · ML-DSAsignatures

The default signature (from CRYSTALS-Dilithium). Replaces ECDSA/RSA signatures for certificates and code signing. Signatures 2,420–4,595 bytes.

FIPS 205 · SLH-DSAbackup signatures

The conservative fallback (from SPHINCS+): security rests only on hash functions — older, battle-tested assumptions — at the price of 7.8–49.9 KB signatures. For firmware and long-lived roots of trust.

Why sizes bite:

A fourth standard (FN-DSA, from Falcon) is in draft, and NIST selected HQC (code-based) in March 2025 as a backup KEM on different maths — deliberate diversity in case lattices ever fall. Sources: Federal Register (official issuance) ↗ wolfSSL explainer ↗ size & parameter guide ↗ NIST on HQC ↗
